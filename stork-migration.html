The White Stork

A story of the white stork

Where Do the
Storks Go?

Every autumn they empty off the rooftops of Europe and disappear. This is the story of where they go, how they get there, and how we finally found out.

Start here. Walk through almost any village in Poland in July and you will see one. A white stork, standing on a great wheel of sticks on top of a chimney, throwing its head back and clattering its beak like a wooden rattle. Now come back in November. The nest is empty. The bird is gone. And for almost all of human history, nobody knew where. That question, where do the storks go, is one of the oldest riddles in the study of nature. Tonight we are going to answer it. And the answer is stranger, and far bigger, than anyone once guessed.

First, meet her properly. The white stork stands over a metre tall, with a wingspan close to two metres, and she weighs about as much as a large house cat. She can live past thirty. And here is the odd thing about her. She is almost completely silent. No song, no call worth the name. When storks meet on the nest they throw their heads right back and clatter their beaks, a sound like someone drumming fast on a wooden fence. If you have ever stood under a stork nest, that clatter is the thing you remember. And a great many of those nests are here. Roughly one white stork in every four is born in Poland, which makes this country, more than any other on earth, the storks' summer home.

The mystery

For centuries, the best minds simply guessed

They sleep through it. Aristotle was sure some birds hibernated, hiding away in hollow trees until the spring returned them.

for ages

They rest in the mud. Fishermen swore that swallows and storks spent the winter asleep at the bottom of ponds and marshes.

1703

They fly to the moon. An English pamphlet worked out the sums and concluded, in all seriousness, that the birds left for the moon and back, a two month trip each way.

Now the mystery itself. A bird that is everywhere all summer and gone all winter. Where does it go? The guesses were wonderful. Aristotle, who got a great many things right, was certain that some birds simply hibernated, tucked away in hollow trees until spring. For centuries after him, ordinary people believed that swallows and storks passed the winter asleep in the mud at the bottom of the lakes. And in 1703, an anonymous English writer did the arithmetic and concluded, with a straight face, that the birds flew to the moon and back, a journey of two months each way. It sounds absurd now. But put yourself in their position. The bird is here, then it is not, and then it is back again. With no way to follow it, the moon is honestly as good a guess as any.

1822  ·  Klütz, Germany

And then, one spring, the answer walked home on its own two legs.

A white stork returned from the winter with a long spear driven clean through its neck. Not a European spear. An iron-tipped African hunting arrow. Somewhere south of the Sahara a hunter had thrown it and missed the kill. The stork survived, and flew thousands of kilometres home with the weapon still in it.

That one wounded bird proved what centuries of philosophy could not: the storks winter in Africa. About 25 of these Pfeilstörche, or "arrow storks", were later recorded. This one still stands in a glass case at the University of Rostock.

The Rostock Pfeilstorch, after Wikimedia

And then, one spring, the answer walked in on its own two legs. Eighteen twenty two, a small country estate near the village of Klütz on Germany's Baltic coast. A white stork arrives home from wherever storks go, and sticking straight through its neck is a spear. Not a European spear. A long African hunting arrow, iron tip, hardwood shaft, exactly the kind used south of the Sahara. Now think about what that actually means. Somewhere in central Africa, a hunter threw that spear and missed the kill. The bird lived. It carried on for months with an arrow through its neck, and then it flew, all the way back, to a German village. They named it the Pfeilstorch, the arrow stork. Around twenty five more of them turned up over the following century. And that single wounded bird did what centuries of clever philosophy never could. It proved, past all argument, that our storks spend their winters in Africa. You can still go and see it. It stands in a glass case at the University of Rostock, spear and all.

The physics of the journey

She doesn't fly to Africa. She soars there.

A stork is too heavy to flap ten thousand kilometres. She would starve. So she waits for a thermal, a column of warm air rising off the sun-baked ground, spreads her wings, and lets it lift her in slow circles, sometimes a kilometre high, without a single beat. Then she glides, losing height gently, until she finds the next one.

Here is the catch that shapes everything: thermals only rise over warm land. The open sea gives nothing to climb. So a soaring stork will go a very long way out of her way rather than cross a wide stretch of water.

So how does she actually do it? Ten thousand kilometres is a very long way to flap, and a stork is heavy. If she beat her wings the whole distance she would burn out and starve long before Africa. So instead she cheats, beautifully. On a sunny day the ground heats up and sends invisible columns of warm rising air up into the sky. We call them thermals. The stork finds one, opens her wings, and simply rides it upward in slow lazy circles, sometimes a full kilometre into the air, without one single flap. Then she peels off the top and glides, losing height gently, until she catches the next elevator up. Circle, glide, circle, glide, the whole way to Africa. But there is a catch, and honestly the whole story hangs on it. Thermals only rise over warm land. The open sea is dead, flat air. So a stork will travel badly out of her way rather than cross a wide stretch of water. Hold onto that one fact. It explains the entire map you are about to see.

The routes

Two roads to Africa

Because no stork will cross the middle of the Mediterranean, Europe's birds pour around its edges instead, and they split into two great streams. Birds from the west drift down through Spain and hop the narrow strait at Gibraltar. Birds from the east sweep through the Balkans and cross at the Bosphorus.

An invisible line runs through central Europe. Which side of it your chimney sits on decides which continent's edge you will follow south. Most of the Polish storks turn east.

Western route Eastern route
Here it is. The whole journey, on a single map. Because no sane stork will cross the middle of the Mediterranean, they pour around the edges of it instead, and Europe splits into two great rivers of birds. Look at the west first. Storks from Spain, France, western Germany drift down through Iberia to the very bottom corner of Europe, and cross into Africa at the Strait of Gibraltar, where the gap is only about fourteen kilometres. A short, nervous hop over water, and they are in Morocco. Now the east. Storks from Poland, from the Baltics, from the Balkans, go the other way. Down through the Balkans to Istanbul, and across into Asia at the Bosphorus, another narrow pinch of water. And running invisibly through the middle of Europe, roughly here, is the divide. Storks on the western side of it turn right, toward Gibraltar. Storks on the eastern side turn left, toward the Bosphorus. Two birds from villages a hundred kilometres apart can end up on different continents. And most of ours, the Polish ones, go east.

Eastern gate

Now, what happens when you funnel an entire continent of birds through two narrow gaps? They pile up. And it is one of the great spectacles in nature. Over the crossing points, the storks gather into slow, spinning towers, thousands of birds all riding a single thermal at the same time, wheeling round and round as they climb. Birdwatchers have a lovely word for it. They call it a kettle, because it looks like water coming to the boil. In the west, at Gibraltar, the birds stack up on the Spanish shore and wait for the right wind before they dare the fourteen kilometres of open sea. But the eastern gate is on another scale entirely. Down the eastern flyway, over the tiny strip of land that is Israel, roughly half a million white storks pour through in a single autumn. Half a million. If you stand in the right valley on the right September day, the sky is nothing but storks, from one horizon to the other.

Now, I have made this sound like a lovely journey, and it is. But I should be honest with you. It is also brutal. Most of the young storks that set off on their very first migration will never come home. The road south is lined with ways to die. The single biggest killer, in most places along the route, is not a predator or the desert. It is us. Power lines. Storks are big birds, and when they land on a pylon or fly into a cable, they are electrocuted or they collide. Then there is the Sahara itself, days on end flying over sand with nothing to eat and nothing to drink, and a single sandstorm or a hard headwind out there can finish a whole flock. At the crossing points, the same great crowds that make the spectacle also make an easy target, and illegal shooting is still a serious problem. And even if she makes it all the way, a dry year in the Sahel means the grasshoppers she came all this way to eat simply are not there. It is a gauntlet, from the first day to the last.

A twist in an ancient story

Some storks have stopped going altogether

In Spain and Portugal, thousands of white storks no longer bother with Africa at all. They have found something better than a ten thousand kilometre round trip: the local landfill. A rubbish dump feeds them right through the winter, so they simply stay, holding their nests all year round.

An instinct older than history, rewritten in a single generation by our garbage. Some of these birds now fly a few kilometres to a dump instead of crossing two continents. Nobody is quite sure yet what that will mean for them.

What changed

Warmer winters and year-round food from open landfills have turned many western storks from long-distance migrants into stay-at-home residents.

Here is where the story takes a strange and very modern turn. In Spain and in Portugal, thousands of white storks have simply stopped going to Africa. They do not migrate at all any more. And the reason is almost funny. They found something better than a ten thousand kilometre round trip full of deserts and power lines and guns. They found the local rubbish dump. An open landfill puts out food all winter long, every day, reliably, so a lot of the western storks now just stay put. They keep their nests all year round, and instead of crossing two continents, they fly a few kilometres to the tip and back. Think about that for a moment. An instinct older than farming, older than writing, one of the deepest rhythms in the natural world, and we have partly rewritten it in a single human generation, with our leftovers. Warmer winters help too. Nobody is entirely sure yet what it will mean for the birds in the long run. But it is happening, right now, in front of us.

How we finally know

From a numbered ring to a dot on a live map

The arrow stork proved they reached Africa. Everything after that, the exact roads, the timings, the individual lives, came from patiently marking birds and getting them back.

1899

The first rings

A Danish schoolteacher, Hans Mortensen, started fitting birds with numbered metal rings. Wherever a bird was found, a piece of the map was filled in.

1900s on

Recoveries

Decades of ringed storks, found and reported across Europe and Africa, slowly drew both flyways and the divide between them.

Today

Satellite tags

Lightweight GPS trackers now report a stork's position from space. We can follow one named bird, day by day, from a Polish chimney to the veld.

So how do we know all of this in such detail? The arrow stork proved they got to Africa. But everything after that, the exact roads, the timings, which bird went where, all of it came from a slow, patient idea: mark the birds, and wait to get them back. It starts in eighteen ninety nine, with a Danish schoolteacher named Hans Mortensen, who began fitting birds with small numbered metal rings, each one unique. The logic is simple. Ring a stork in Denmark, and if somebody finds it in Sudan and reports the number, you have just drawn a line on the map that no one could draw before. Do that for decades, with thousands of birds, and the two great flyways slowly appear out of the dots, along with the invisible divide between them. And today it is almost magical. We fit a stork with a tiny GPS tag, lighter than a phone, and it reports its position from space. We can now follow one single named bird, day by day, hour by hour, all the way from a chimney in Poland to a waterhole in South Africa, and watch the whole ten thousand kilometres unfold on a screen.

The homecoming

The same nest. Often the same mate.

After all of that, the crossings, the desert, a whole African winter, the stork comes back. Not just to Poland, or to the right district. To the exact same nest, the same wheel of sticks on the same chimney, often to meet the same partner she left in the autumn.

The male usually arrives first, a few days ahead, to hold the nest. Across Europe his arrival is the real first day of spring, a bird that carries luck onto your roof. It is why the storks are painted on the barns and stitched into the folk tales.

And then, after all of that, the crossings, the desert, a whole winter on another continent, she comes back. And this is the part that gets me every time. She does not just come back to Poland, or even to the right district. She comes back to the exact same nest. The same wheel of sticks, on the same chimney, on the same house. And very often she comes back to the same partner she said goodbye to in the autumn, the two of them navigating separately across ten thousand kilometres and meeting again on the same roof. The male usually turns up first, a few days early, to claim the nest and repair it before she arrives. And all across Europe, for hundreds of years, that arrival has meant one thing. It is the real first day of spring. A stork on your roof is luck, it is new life, it is the year turning. That is why they are painted on the barns, and carved over the doorways, and woven right through the folk tales. The bird earned that place. It flew to Africa and back to keep it.

Epilogue

One bird ties a Polish village to the African plain

The stork clattering on that chimney in July has stood over a waterhole in South Africa. She has crossed the Sahara twice, threaded the Bosphorus, and found her way home to a wheel of sticks she built herself. Look up this summer. You are watching one of the longest, oldest commutes on earth.

Keep the nests up. Bury the power lines where you can. Then get out of the way and let them fly.

Safe travels.

So let me leave you with this. The next time you see a white stork clattering away on a chimney on a July afternoon, looking for all the world like a fixture of the village, remember where those feet have been. That same bird has stood over a waterhole in South Africa. She has crossed the Sahara, not once but twice. She has threaded the eye of the Bosphorus in a tower of ten thousand birds, and then she has found her way back, across ten thousand kilometres, to a pile of sticks she built with her own beak, on your roof. One bird, tying a Polish village to the plains of Africa, every single year, quietly, while we mostly do not notice. Our job in all of this is small and it is simple. Keep the nests up. Bury the power lines where we can. And then get out of the way, and let them fly. Safe travels.
